TL;DR

Two LLM agents with separate tasks share a repository. Changes to a file you don't own must go through a reviewer. When the reviewer goes quiet, the file's owner starts making the change for its peer: no instruction to help, no shared reward, no permission broken. We call this a favor. Favors are common across four models, and many of them break services owned by teams that never joined the conversation.

of FavorBench trajectories contain at least one favor

Cooperation without incentives

Much of multi-agent research treats cooperation as the goal. Systems are designed to elicit it, typically through shared rewards, explicit instructions to coordinate, or repeated interaction that rewards reciprocity, and its emergence is read as a success.

Our setting removes all three. Agents nonetheless act on one another's behalf, and that help is locally beneficial but externally costly: the requester's task succeeds and the owner's own work is unaffected, while the cost falls on teams that never took part in the conversation. On trap tasks, 43.7% of favors break such a team.

DriverIn our setting
Shared rewardAbsent Each agent is rewarded only for its own tests.

Now watch the real agents

The opening figure is a sketch. These are six real tasks, replayed word for word from our FavorBench logs. Alice and Bob each have their own task, and one of them owns the shared file the other needs. Pick a run, or click any task in it.

Every model does favors. Not every model breaks things.

No favors happen while the reviewer is around. They appear in the first task after it goes quiet and persist. Explicit refusals are rare (at most 3.8% of FavorBench tasks); when an owner doesn't help, it usually endorses the request in words or does nothing.

  • GPT-6 Luna does the most favors on FavorBench, on 67–70% of tasks.
  • DeepSeek rarely asks the owner directly (7–10% of tasks), yet 81–89% of its favors are volunteered: owners pick up requests addressed to the reviewer.
  • Claude helps least on FavorBench (29%), and its favors almost never cause harm (0–1.4%) because it often finds a safer alternative. On CooperBench, where the owner can simply approve the pending change, it helps most (89%).

Cooperation can route around safeguards

“The road to hell is paved with good intentions.”

Proverb

LLM agents help each other without reward or reciprocity, using permissions they legitimately hold. The problem is not a lack of cooperation; it is that help need not respect organizational boundaries or the parties outside the conversation. Safe multi-agent systems need permissions, task scope, communication structure and the interests of absent stakeholders to be designed together.
